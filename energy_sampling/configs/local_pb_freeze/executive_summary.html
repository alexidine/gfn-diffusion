Executive summary · crystal sampler · 7 September 2026

Should the reverse process keep learning after the warm-up?

No. Once the warm-up stage is done, freezing it costs nothing we can measure and removes a slow oscillation that the trainable version drags the whole model through. But it has to be frozen properly: the obvious cheap way makes things worse.

Recommendation. After the warm-up stage, stop training the reverse process entirely, including the shared network layers it reads from. A config switch for this now exists (freeze_backward_policy: full). If a smaller change is preferred, the fallback is to stop the reverse process learning from the replay memory only.

Do not freeze it by simply switching off its own parameters. That looks frozen on the dashboard and behaves worse than leaving it alone.

The setup in plain terms

The sampler builds a crystal structure step by step, starting from pure noise and adding structure over a fixed number of steps until a candidate crystal comes out. Call that the generator. Training it requires a second, mirror-image rule that takes a finished crystal and dissolves it back into noise, one step at a time. Call that the reverse process. The training objective is a balance condition: the probability of building a crystal along a path must match the probability of un-building it along the same path, weighted by how good the crystal is.

A textbook reverse process exists (a random walk pulled back toward the origin), and it needs no training. We instead let the reverse process learn small adjustments to that textbook rule, within fixed limits, because on hard energy surfaces this lets the generator get away with fewer steps. Training runs in two stages: a warm-up in which both processes are fit to a library of known good crystals, and a long main stage in which the generator is pushed toward the energy landscape using fresh samples, the library, and a replay memory of its own recent attempts. The question was whether the reverse process should still be learning during that main stage.

What we found

  • The reverse process is only allowed to reshape the schedule, not the destination. It can speed up or slow down the pull toward noise per coordinate, and widen or narrow the noise per coordinate, by bounded amounts. It cannot move the path sideways or couple coordinates. The warm-up uses that freedom heavily: after warm-up its paths are about 24 units of log-probability away from the textbook rule.
  • Its main benefit shrinks with the number of steps. The freedom exists to make the generator's job representable when steps are large. We now run 100 steps rather than 10. A scaling argument puts the remaining benefit at least ten times smaller than when the design was made, and probably a hundred.
  • In the main stage it keeps moving, and the replay memory is doing much of the pushing. On the production runs its gradient stays at 10 to 40 percent of the generator's for the whole run. In the local test, 43 percent of that push came from the replay memory, where the reverse process can lower the training error by simply agreeing with stored past attempts instead of the generator improving.
  • Frozen, nothing measurable was lost. In a controlled side-by-side (same starting point, same random seed, 3,200 steps), the generator's fit, its coverage of the library, the spread of its importance weights, and the memorisation sensor were all identical to the trainable version.
  • Frozen, a limit cycle disappeared. With the reverse process trainable, the model runs a clean 600-step oscillation: it alternately over-explains and under-explains the replay memory, and the generator's step-noise and fit ride along with it. Frozen, that trace is flat.
  • The cheap freeze is the worst option. The reverse process shares its first layers with the generator. Switching off only its own parameters leaves those shared layers being pushed by the reverse process's errors, with no way left to correct them. That run had the worst generator fit of the three and the one signature we did not want: stored attempts became more probable than fresh ones.

Why this makes sense

With the reverse process fixed, the balance condition has exactly one generator that satisfies it. With the reverse process free, there is a whole family of matching pairs and the training objective has no preference among them; the freedom is only useful if it lets the generator represent something it otherwise could not. That representability gap closes quickly as the number of steps grows, and the production runs now use ten times more steps than the setting in which the freedom was found useful. What remains is a second, smaller role: keeping the reverse process tuned to the generator lowers the noise of the training signal. Over the 3,200 steps tested, that noise did not measurably grow when the reverse process was frozen.

What this does not settle

Longer runs
3,200 steps locally against 50,000 on the cluster. Whether a frozen reverse process eventually becomes a noise problem is open; there was no sign of it here.
100-step paths
The local test used 10 steps. Every argument says the frozen version does better with more steps, but that is the direction, not a measurement.
Other energy models
One system, one energy model, one learning rate, one seed. The disappearance of the oscillation is a clean qualitative difference; the small level differences are not.
Crystal energies
The energy readout at 500 samples swings too much within a single run to rank the three.

What happens next

Set freeze_backward_policy: full in the next production battery and watch the same three things: the memory gap should stay flat near zero, the importance-weight spread should not trend upward, and the reverse process's gradient should read exactly zero. If the spread does climb over tens of thousands of steps, the right response is a periodic re-tune of the reverse process using the warm-up objective, not returning it to the main training loss.
