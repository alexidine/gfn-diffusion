## Q1. Hirshfeld (1968): what Cheshire groups are, their purpose, relation to Euclidean normalisers, and the "Cheshire cell"

### Takeaway
Hirshfeld (1968) introduced "Cheshire groups" explicitly as a tool for **trial-structure generation**: the group of transformations that leave the set of equivalent positions (the symmetry-element framework) of a space group invariant; its direct product with the molecular point group gives the symmetry of the 6-D (orientation + position) trial-structure parameter space, and the **asymmetric unit of that 6-D space is the region to be scanned**. ITA later identifies Hirshfeld's Cheshire groups with the Euclidean normalisers (for general metric). This is the closest prior art to the requester's construction (minus the lattice-parameter factor).

### Cited Findings
- Citation: Hirshfeld, F. L. (1968). "Symmetry in the generation of trial structures." *Acta Cryst.* A24, 301–311. The paper includes a table of Cheshire cells for all 230 space groups. [S] — [CCP4 "Cheshire cells" page](https://smb.slac.stanford.edu/facilities/software/ccp4/html/cheshirecell.html). DOI believed to be 10.1107/S0567739468000574 [K, unverified].
- Abstract content (as returned by search, paraphrase/quotation from the abstract record) [S]: "The set of transformations of the crystal axes that leave invariant the coordinates of equivalent positions for a given space group defines a corresponding derivative symmetry, conforming to one of thirty distinct 'Cheshire' groups. The direct product of this group with the molecular point group specifies the symmetry of the six-dimensional space of the trial-structure parameters. The asymmetric unit in this space is the region to be scanned by the several parameters." — [synapsesocial record W1987710456 via search](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W1987710456) (page itself returned HTTP 429; text came from the search-engine summary of it). Note the "thirty distinct" count refers to Cheshire-group *types* as Hirshfeld classified them; verify against the original.
- Informal definition used in the MR community: the Cheshire group is "the space group of the crystal with its material contents removed, keeping only the symmetry elements" (Ian Tickle); the name is a joke on the Cheshire Cat's grin remaining after the cat vanishes. [V] — [CCP4 Cheshire cells page](https://smb.slac.stanford.edu/facilities/software/ccp4/html/cheshirecell.html).
- **Cheshire cell** (AMoRe documentation quoted by CCP4): "The Cheshire cell is the minimum volume which will allow a unique solution" — for the *first* molecule's translation search it runs from one permissible origin to the next; e.g. P2₁2₁2₁: 0–½, 0–½, 0–½; P2₁: 0–½, any y, 0–½. For subsequent molecules the origin is already fixed so the search is the whole primitive cell. In P1 the Cheshire cell is a single point (no translation search for one molecule). [V] — [CCP4 Cheshire cells page](https://smb.slac.stanford.edu/facilities/software/ccp4/html/cheshirecell.html).
- Hirshfeld's table uses notation such as "Z" for a primitive cell with vanishing axes and "eb" ("no b": continuous/arbitrary along b, i.e. polar direction). [V] — same CCP4 page.
- ITA (Koch & Fischer, Part 15 in the 2005/2006 edition) states that the Euclidean normalisers of the space groups were first derived by Hirshfeld (1968) under the name "Cheshire groups"; later tabulated by Gubler and by Fischer & Koch; Euclidean normalisers of triclinic and monoclinic groups with specialised metric determined by Koch & Müller (1990). [S] — search summaries of [ITA Vol. A (2006) ch. 15.1](https://it.iucr.org/Ab/ch15o1v0001) (page 403 to fetch tool).
- Kowiel, Jaskolski & Dauter (2014) use "anti-Cheshire symmetry" as a name for the **chirality-preserving Euclidean normaliser** ("all symmetry operations of the first kind that map all symmetry elements of the original space group onto themselves") and the "anti-Cheshire unit cell", whose asymmetric unit defines the uniqueness region for placing a macromolecular model. [S for the quoted definition; V for bibliographic record] — Kowiel, M., Jaskolski, M. & Dauter, Z. (2014). "ACHESYM: an algorithm and server for standardized placement of macromolecular models in the unit cell." *Acta Cryst.* D70, 3290–3298. [doi:10.1107/S1399004714024572](https://doi.org/10.1107/S1399004714024572); [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=ACHESYM%20anti-Cheshire&resultType=core&format=json).

### Inferences
- Hirshfeld's 6-D construction (Cheshire group × molecular point group, scan its asymmetric unit) is essentially the requester's "normaliser AU × orientation fundamental zone" for a *fixed* lattice. The requester's addition is the lattice factor (reduced cell) and the explicit chirality restriction.
- Terminological trap: Hirshfeld says "asymmetric unit" of the 6-D parameter space, not "fundamental domain".
- The "Cheshire cell" of MR practice is a *cell* (parallelepiped from origin to next permissible origin), i.e. the translation-lattice quotient of the normaliser's translations; it is generally larger than the asymmetric unit of the full Euclidean normaliser, because it ignores the normaliser's non-translational (e.g. inversion, rotation) elements. Kowiel et al.'s "asymmetric unit of the anti-Cheshire cell" is the finer object.

### Gaps
- Exact Hirshfeld wording, whether he used "Cheshire cell" himself or whether the term originates with AMoRe/Navaza, and the exact meaning of "thirty" could not be checked (full text inaccessible).
- Whether Hirshfeld's Cheshire groups coincide with Euclidean normalisers in every case (e.g. treatment of specialised metrics, the "Z" vanishing-axis cases) was not verified from the primary text; ITA's statement is relayed via search summary.

## Q2. Koch, Fischer & Müller on normalisers in International Tables Vol. A

### Takeaway
The normaliser material is ITA Vol. A Part 15 (Koch & Fischer) in the 1st–5th editions (1983–2005; reprinted 2006) and Chapter 3.5 (Koch, Fischer & Müller), pp. 826–851, in the 6th edition (2016, ed. Aroyo). It defines Euclidean and affine normalisers, gives tables for general and specialised metric, gives chirality-preserving Euclidean normalisers (Tables 3.5.2.3–3.5.2.5 in 2016), and explains Euclidean-equivalent descriptions of a crystal structure; the number of such descriptions equals the index of G in N_E(G).

- Specialised metric: Bilbao's NORMALIZER offers three modes: "Euclidean (general metric)", "Enhanced Euclidean (specialized metric)", "Affine". - Counting equivalent descriptions: the cosets of G in N_E(G) generate the different Euclidean-equivalent descriptions of a structure; the number of equivalent descriptions equals the index [N_E(G) : G]. Implemented by Bilbao EQUIVSTRU, which "transforms the corresponding structure with the elements of the euclidean normalizer of the space group." [S/V] — [Bilbao Equivalent Structures help](https://cryst.ehu.es/cryst/equivstru.html); [de la Flor, BCS workshop 2022 slides "Crystal-structure descriptions"](https://www.cryst.ehu.es/html/resources/bcs_workshop2022/delaFlor_Crystal-structure%20tools_Crystal-structure%20descriptions.pdf); worked example CsCl in Pm-3m with Cs/Cl swapped between 1a/1b.

(1983), "On the equivalence of point configurations due to Euclidean normalizers (Cheshire groups) of space groups", *Acta Cryst.* A39, 907–915 (introduces/uses **Wyckoff sets** = Wyckoff positions conjugate under the affine normaliser); Burzlaff, H. & Zimmermann, H. [S, appeared in search results via a Scribd copy and OUP chapter listing](https://academic.oup.com/book/59051/chapter/496287812).

### Inferences
- For the requester's purposes, the correct ITA names are: **Euclidean normaliser N_E(G)** (general metric), **Euclidean normaliser for specialised metric**, **chirality-preserving Euclidean normaliser N_E⁺(G)**, **affine normaliser N_A(G)**. "Cheshire group" is the historical synonym for N_E(G) (and "anti-Cheshire" is a niche MX term for N_E⁺).
- Because N_E(G) can grow at specialised metrics (e.g. a = c in monoclinic, a = b in orthorhombic), a "reduced cell × N_E-AU" product domain is not a clean Cartesian product on the measure-zero metric boundaries; the coordinate-space quotient is by a larger group there.
- For polar groups N_E(G) contains continuous translations, so its "asymmetric unit" is lower-dimensional in those directions — fixing the polar origin (as the requester does) is the standard remedy, mirrored by Hirshfeld's "vanishing axis" notation and moyo's continuous-direction field.

### Gaps
- Exact edition in which "chirality-preserving Euclidean normalizer" first appeared in ITA (likely 5th ed. 2002/2005 Part 15, possibly earlier) — not verified.
- ITA's exact wording on enumerating equivalent descriptions (and its caveat that additional descriptions arise for specialised metrics and from the affine normaliser when the metric is not fixed) not read directly.

## Q3. Is the "asymmetric unit of the Euclidean normaliser" named/tabulated, or used to restrict a search or define a unique representative?

### Takeaway
Yes, in three bodies of work: Hirshfeld (1968) (AU of Cheshire group × molecular point group as the trial-structure search region); macromolecular MR (Cheshire cell; Chirikjian's motion-space papers); and Kowiel et al. (2014) ACHESYM (AU of the anti-Cheshire = chirality-preserving normaliser cell as a standard placement region). ITA tabulates normalisers, not their asymmetric units; I found no general tabulation of N_E-asymmetric units for all 230 groups.

### Cited Findings
- Hirshfeld (1968): "The asymmetric unit in this space is the region to be scanned." [S] — see Q1.
- ACHESYM: converts alternative descriptions to a standard unique setting; uniqueness regions "are related to the asymmetric unit of the anti-Cheshire unit cell"; also finds a compact assembly when several chains are present; inputs PDB/mmCIF, can reindex reflections and modify phases. [S quote / V abstract paraphrase] — [Kowiel, Jaskolski & Dauter 2014, doi:10.1107/S1399004714024572](https://doi.org/10.1107/S1399004714024572).
- Chirikjian and co-workers formalise MR search spaces as cosets/double cosets of SE(3) by space groups and discuss normalisers in this context ("motion spaces", fundamental domains). [S] — search results: [Chirikjian, "Mathematical aspects of molecular replacement II: geometry"](https://rpk.lcsr.jhu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/Chirikjian12_Math-Aspects-II-Geometry.pdf), ["Kinematics meets crystallography"](https://rpk.lcsr.jhu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/04/Chirikjian15_Kinematics-Meets-Crystallography.pdf), ["Measure theoretic decompositions of motion spaces"](https://rpk.lcsr.jhu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/Measure-theoretic-decompositions-of-motion-spaces.pdf), ["Decomposition of Sohncke space groups into product of Bieberbach and symmorphic parts"](https://rpk.lcsr.jhu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/Decomposition-of-Sohncke-space-groups-into-product-of-Bieberbach-and-symmorphic-parts.pdf). Journal refs [K]: Chirikjian, G. S. (2011) *Acta Cryst.* A67, 435–446 (Part I, algebraic); Chirikjian & Yan (2012) *Acta Cryst.* A68, 208–221 (Part II, geometry); Chirikjian, Ratnayake & Sajjadi (2015) *Acta Cryst.* A71, 186–194 (Part III); Chirikjian, Sajjadi, Toptygin & Yan (2015) *Acta Cryst.* A71 (Part IV) / later measure-theoretic paper.
- Parthé–Gelato standardisation selects, among descriptions differing by origin choice and rotated/inverted axes (i.e. normaliser cosets), the one minimising a standardisation parameter Γ (sum of origin-to-site distances over a chosen asymmetric unit, computed in fractional coordinates as if the cell were a unit cube); a second parameter (centre of gravity) breaks ties. This is a unique-representative rule over normaliser cosets, though not phrased as an "asymmetric unit of the normaliser". [S] — search summaries of [Parthé & Gelato (1984) *Acta Cryst.* A40, 169–183](https://journals.iucr.org/a/issues/1984/a1/00/a49312/a49312.pdf) and [Gelato & Parthé (1987) *J. Appl. Cryst.* 20, 139–143, STRUCTURE TIDY](https://journals.iucr.org/a/issues/1987/a1/00/a44614/a44614.pdf) (note: the 1987 URL found is an Acta A path; verify which paper it is).

### Inferences
- The requester's "AU of N_E" is genuinely prior art for translational search (Hirshfeld; MR), and for unique placement (ACHESYM). Combining it with a reduced-cell domain and an orientation fundamental zone into a single fundamental domain for molecular-crystal parameter space is not something I found stated in one source.
- A defensible naming: "asymmetric unit of the (chirality-preserving) Euclidean normaliser" (ITA vocabulary), with "Cheshire cell"/"Cheshire group" as historical synonyms.

### Gaps
- No exhaustive search of CSP literature was done (out of scope); I found no source tabulating N_E-AUs for all 230 groups. Grosse-Kunstleve et al. (2011) "exact direct-space asymmetric units for the 230 space groups" (*Acta Cryst.* A67, 269–275 [K]; [IUCr page](https://journals.iucr.org/a/issues/2011/03/00/pz5088/index.html), 403) tabulates space-group AUs only, as far as I know [K].

## Q4. Structure seminvariants / permissible origins and software implementations

### Takeaway
Permissible origins (Hauptman & Karle) are the translational part of the Euclidean normaliser; structure seminvariants are quantities invariant under permissible origin shifts. cctbx/sgtbx computes seminvariant vectors and moduli (Grosse-Kunstleve 1999) and uses normaliser generators in EMMA; Bilbao provides NORMALIZER, WYCKSETS, EQUIVSTRU, COMPSTRU, SETSTRU; spglib documents that its standardisation is unique only up to N_E; moyo computes N_E, N_E⁺ and continuous directions; STRUCTURE TIDY (in PLATON) implements Parthé–Gelato.

### Cited Findings
- Grosse-Kunstleve, R. W. (1999). "Algorithms for deriving crystallographic space-group information." *Acta Cryst.* A55, 383–395. Abstract: structure-seminvariant vectors and moduli represent permissible origin shifts; steps include determining point group, selecting generators, determining continuous allowed origin shifts, then discrete shifts; all computations use symmetry operations alone, without a metric. [S] — [bohrium record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/algorithms-for-deriving-crystallographic-space-group-information/814549659967029248-13687); [cctbx reprint list](https://cci.lbl.gov/publications/download/sh5006_reprint.pdf).
- cctbx `sgtbx::structure_seminvariants` computes these vectors/moduli and separates discrete from continuous shifts. [S] — [seminvariant.h mirror](https://genomics-lab.fleming.gr/fleming/tools/molprobity/modules/cctbx_project/cctbx/sgtbx/seminvariant.h) (third-party mirror of cctbx).
- cctbx EMMA (`euclidean_model_matching`) finds the isometry matching the largest subset of one model onto another, using flags `use_normalizer_k2l` / `use_normalizer_l2n` for normaliser cosets. [S] — [cctbx.euclidean_model_matching docs](https://cctbx.github.io/cctbx/cctbx.euclidean_model_matching.html); [cctbxbb list post](https://phenix-online.org/pipermail/cctbxbb/2013-March/000705.html). The `addl_generators_of_euclidean_normalizer` method name (sgtbx space_group_type) is [K, not verified]; its naming follows ITA's "Additional generators" columns [S].
- Grosse-Kunstleve, R. W. & Adams, P. D. (2003). "On symmetries of substructures." *Acta Cryst.* D59, 1966–1973: groups allowed origin shifts / seminvariant vectors with alternative origin choices and hand ambiguity under "expected symmetry". [S] — [IUCr PDF](https://journals.iucr.org/d/issues/2003/11/00/ba5051/ba5051.pdf).

### Inferences
- Permissible origins = translations in N_E(G) modulo lattice translations (plus continuous ones for polar groups); the Cheshire cell is the fundamental cell of that translation group. The full N_E(G) adds inversions/rotations (e.g. change of hand in Sohncke groups is excluded by using N_E⁺).

## Q5. Lattice/cell reduction as a fundamental domain

### Takeaway
The Andrews–Bernstein line explicitly uses "fundamental region"/"fundamental unit" for the Niggli-reduced cone in G6 and the Selling-reduced region in S6; the older reduction literature (Niggli, Buerger, Delaunay, Gruber, Křivý–Gruber) mostly speaks of "reduced cells" and "reduction conditions". In mathematics, the Minkowski reduction domain is a fundamental domain for GL(n,ℤ) acting on positive-definite quadratic forms.

### Cited Findings
- Andrews & Bernstein (2012/2014), "The geometry of Niggli reduction": 216 boundary polytopes of the Niggli cone in G6 — 15 5-D boundaries, 53 4-D intersections, etc.; separate all-acute and all-obtuse branches. BGAOL "sews" separate regions of "the G6 fundamental region" into one region preserving distances. [S] — [PMC3937813 (Andrews & Bernstein 2014, *J. Appl. Cryst.* 47, 346–359 [K for page numbers])](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3937813); [ar5iv 1305.6561](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1305.6561).
- A 2019 *Acta Cryst.* A paper notes "the fundamental unit" of Niggli-reduced G6 space is non-convex, motivating Selling-reduced S6 whose fundamental region is convex. [S] — [journals.iucr.org ae5061](https://journals.iucr.org/a/issues/2019/03/00/ae5061/ae5061.pdf) (Andrews, Bernstein & Sauter 2019, *Acta Cryst.* A75, 593–599 [K]).
- Original G6: Andrews, L. C. & Bernstein, H. J. (1988) "Lattices and reduced cells as points in 6-space and selection of Bravais lattice type by projections", *Acta Cryst.* A44, 1009–1018. [S: cited by later papers in search results; page numbers K].
- Other reduction references [K, unverified]: Niggli, P. (1928) *Handbuch der Experimentalphysik* 7, Part 1; Buerger, M. J. (1957) *Z. Kristallogr.* 109, 42–60; Delaunay, B. (1933) *Z. (1976) *Acta Cryst.* A32, 297–298; Grosse-Kunstleve, Sauter & Adams (2004) *Acta Cryst.* A60, 1–6 (numerically stable reduction, in cctbx); ITA (2016) ch. 3.1 (Burzlaff, Grimmer, Gruber, de Wolff, Zimmermann) on reduced bases.

### Inferences
- The requester's "reduced cell" factor is properly named "the Niggli-reduced region (Niggli cone) of G6" or "Selling/Delaunay-reduced region of S6", with "fundamental region" already used by Andrews–Bernstein. Note the Niggli cone is a fundamental domain for GL(3,ℤ) only up to boundary identifications (hence BGAOL's boundary transforms).

## Q6. Orientation space: fundamental zone / asymmetric domain of SO(3)

### Takeaway
Texture/EBSD literature uses "fundamental zone" (Rodrigues–Frank space) and "fundamental region" (MTEX `fundamentalRegion`, `project2FundamentalRegion`); MX rotation-function literature speaks of "asymmetric units" of rotation space ("rotation-function space groups").

### Cited Findings
- MTEX: restricting to the "fundamental region" removes the multiplicity of rotation matrices for one orientation; its geometry differs from that of orientation space, so nearby orientations can map to distant representatives. Misorientation regions via `fundamentalRegion(cs1, cs2)`; antipodal symmetry halves the region; cubic–cubic disorientation zone is 1/48 of the cubic orientation fundamental zone. [S] — [MTEX OrientationFundamentalRegion](https://mtex-toolbox.github.io/OrientationFundamentalRegion.html); [MTEX Embeddings](https://mtex-toolbox.github.io/OrientationEmbeddings.html); [Wikipedia: Misorientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misorientation).
- orix (pyxem) reduces (mis)orientations to the Rodrigues fundamental zone; convention change sym_B·m·sym_A under discussion. [S] — [orix PR 442](https://github.com/pyxem/orix/pull/442).
- Primary refs [K, unverified]: Frank, F. C. (1988) "Orientation mapping", *Metall. Trans. A* 19, 403–408 (Rodrigues–Frank space); Heinz, A. & Neumann, P. (1991) *Acta Cryst.* A47, 780–789 (asymmetric domains / fundamental zones for cubic, hexagonal, tetragonal, orthorhombic); Morawiec, A. & Field, D. P. (1996) *Phil. Mag. ## Q7. Terminology: "fundamental domain/region" vs "asymmetric unit"

### Takeaway
Crystallographers say **asymmetric unit**; mathematicians say **fundamental domain/region** (Schoenflies' *Fundamentalbereich*). They are treated as near-synonyms in secondary sources, and ITA's AU definition (smallest closed part of space that fills space under the group) matches a closed fundamental domain. "Fundamental region" is used in crystallographic texts for lattice reduction (Andrews–Bernstein) and in texture analysis; orbifold literature frames AUs as orbifolds.

### Cited Findings
- LibreTexts equates the crystallographic "asymmetric unit" with the mathematical "fundamental region"/"fundamental domain". [S, secondary teaching source] — [LibreTexts Asymmetric unit](https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Inorganic_Chemistry/Supplemental_Modules_(Inorganic_Chemistry)/Crystallography/Fundamental_Crystallography/Asymmetric_unit).
- An arXiv paper defines an AU as a minimal, closed, simply connected subset of a unit cell whose images under all symmetry operations tile space. [S] — [arXiv 2510.13746](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13746).
- Orbifolds as an alternative to fundamental domains: each orbifold represents one asymmetric domain of the pattern; credits Conway & Huson (2002) and Conway et al. (2008). ### Inferences
- A materials scientist will likely read "fundamental domain" as "asymmetric unit" of the space group acting on Cartesian/fractional space, *not* as a domain in the space of crystal descriptions. Using it for "reduced cell × N_E-AU × orientation fundamental zone" is mathematically correct (fundamental domain of the group of description-changes acting on parameter space) but should be explicitly defined, citing Hirshfeld for the precedent and naming each factor in ITA vocabulary.
- Suggested naming: "fundamental domain of the description space", composed of (i) Niggli/Selling-reduced region (Andrews–Bernstein "fundamental region"), (ii) asymmetric unit of the chirality-preserving Euclidean normaliser N_E⁺(G) (Cheshire group / anti-Cheshire group), with polar continuous translations fixed, (iii) fundamental zone of SO(3) under the molecular point group (texture-analysis term).
