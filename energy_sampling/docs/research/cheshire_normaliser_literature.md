# Hirshfeld charted the domain; modern CSP forgot it

*Research note. Not repository policy and not a description of the code.*

*Literature scan, 2026-11-17. Question: who names and who exploits Cheshire-group / Euclidean-normaliser redundancy in crystal search and generative models, and what to call each factor of the crystal-parameter fundamental domain. Compiled by agents from web sources; evidence levels [V]/[S]/[K] as defined below. Per-topic source notes are in `cheshire_normaliser_literature_sources/`.*

The position-and-orientation part of your fundamental domain has a published precedent: **Hirshfeld (1968) defined "Cheshire groups", today called Euclidean normalisers, specifically for trial-structure generation**. He showed that the direct product of the Cheshire group with the molecular point group is the symmetry of the six-dimensional position-plus-orientation space, and wrote: "The asymmetric unit in this space is the region to be scanned" ([archived IUCr abstract](http://web.archive.org/web/20250423082147/https://journals.iucr.org/paper?S0567739468000501)). Three parts of your construction are not in that abstract: the reduced-cell factor, the explicit chirality-preserving restriction, and the dependence of the normaliser on specialised metric. No modern molecular CSP code we checked, and no generative model, samples on a normaliser-quotiented domain. GLEE, PyXtal, Genarris, USPEX, AIRSS and the powder direct-space codes sample the whole cell (or at most the space group's asymmetric unit) over all of SO(3), then deduplicate after minimisation. Molecular replacement (MR) is the only living community that bounds a search with the normaliser. It does so only for translation, only for the first molecule, and only over the "Cheshire cell". Machine-learning (ML) papers use the normaliser solely to enumerate or augment equivalent descriptions (Wyckoff Transformer, Yamazaki et al., MaskGXT). The defensible terms are: **reduced-cell (Niggli) region**, **asymmetric unit of the (chirality-preserving) Euclidean normaliser N_E(G) / N_E⁺(G)**, **origin fixed along polar (floating) directions**, and **orientation fundamental zone**. Cite Hirshfeld for the composite idea, and use "Cheshire cell" only in its MR sense, which is coarser than the normaliser asymmetric unit.

Evidence levels carried from the notes: **[V]** means read in the primary source, authoritative documentation or code during the research. **[S]** means taken from a secondary source or search summary only. **[K]** means background knowledge not checked. The notes' [CODE]/[PAPER] labels map to [V], [SECONDARY] to [S], and [PRIOR] to [K]. Anything below [V] must be checked before it is cited in a paper.

## Hirshfeld's 1968 Cheshire group is ITA's Euclidean normaliser

Hirshfeld's verified abstract defines the Cheshire group as the derivative symmetry of the transformations of the crystal axes that leave the coordinates of a space group's equivalent positions invariant. It says these fall into **thirty distinct types** [V] ([archived abstract](http://web.archive.org/web/20250423082147/https://journals.iucr.org/paper?S0567739468000501)). The paper fixes the cell and the molecule's geometry. Its subject is the scan over position and orientation, not the lattice. The correct DOI is **10.1107/S0567739468000501** [V] ([Scilit](https://www.scilit.net/publications/e7c1a4bfa847690ff23f48dd8cf76c5d)). One note carried an unverified alternative, S0567739468000574, which should be discarded. The full text could not be opened. Four points are therefore unverified: whether Hirshfeld coined "Cheshire cell", what the count of thirty is based on, whether his groups include improper (hand-reversing) operations, and whether he tabulated the 6-D asymmetric units. CCP4 reproduces a Hirshfeld-derived table of Cheshire groups and cells for all 230 space groups; for example, P2₁2₁2₁ maps to Cheshire group Pmmm with cell ½a × ½b × ½c, and P1's Cheshire cell is a point [V] ([CCP4 Cheshire cells](https://staraniso.globalphasing.org/html/cheshirecell.html)).

The identification with modern normaliser theory is explicit in the MR literature. **Dauter (2013) states that the Cheshire symmetry corresponds to the Euclidean normaliser**, citing Fischer & Koch (1983) and Koch & Fischer (2006). He also notes that most Cheshire groups are centrosymmetric, except those carrying a single screw handedness (3₁, 4₁, 6₁ and relatives) [V] ([Dauter 2013, PMC4005360](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4005360/)). That centrosymmetry is why a chirality restriction is needed at all. The authoritative tabulation is International Tables Vol. A (ITA): Part 15 (Koch & Fischer) in the 1983–2005 editions, and **ch. 3.5, "Normalizers of space groups and their use in crystallography" (Koch, Fischer & Müller), pp. 826–851, in the 2016 6th edition** [V for the citation as given by Bilbao] ([Bilbao NORMALIZER](https://cryst.ehu.es/cryst/get_nor.html); [ITA ch. 3.5 contents](https://it.iucr.org/Ac/ch3o5v0001/contents/)). Search summaries say ch. 3.5 tabulates Euclidean and **chirality-preserving Euclidean normalisers** (Tables 3.5.2.3–3.5.2.5) [S]. They also say Koch & Müller (1990) derived the larger normalisers that triclinic and monoclinic groups acquire at specialised metric [S] ([ITA 2006 ch. 15.1](https://it.iucr.org/Ab/ch15o1v0001)). Bilbao's NORMALIZER exposes all three variants (general-metric Euclidean, "enhanced" specialised-metric Euclidean, and affine) [V] ([Bilbao](https://cryst.ehu.es/cryst/get_nor.html)). The Rust library moyo computes N_E(G), the chirality-preserving subgroup via `preserve_chirality`, and a `continuous_translation_directions` field. That field is non-empty for polar groups, where N_E is finite only modulo those continuous translations [V] ([moyo normalizer.rs](https://docs.rs/crate/moyo/0.10.0/source/src/identify/normalizer.rs)).

Three facts from this theory bear directly on your domain. First, **the number of Euclidean-equivalent descriptions of one structure equals the index [N_E(G) : G]**; Bilbao's EQUIVSTRU generates them from the normaliser's coset representatives [S/V] ([EQUIVSTRU](https://cryst.ehu.es/cryst/equivstru.html); [de la Flor BCS slides](https://www.cryst.ehu.es/html/resources/bcs_workshop2022/delaFlor_Crystal-structure%20tools_Crystal-structure%20descriptions.pdf)). Yamazaki et al. count **8 normaliser cosets for P-1** [V] ([arXiv 2503.16784](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16784)). Second, permissible origins, the object of Hauptman–Karle seminvariant theory, are the translation part of N_E(G). cctbx computes these as structure-seminvariant vectors and moduli without a metric, following Grosse-Kunstleve (1999) [S] ([cctbx reprint](https://cci.lbl.gov/publications/download/sh5006_reprint.pdf)). Spglib documents that its standardisation is unique only up to normaliser generators [S] ([spglib definition.rst mirror](https://grok.dragonflybsd.org/source/xref/dports/science/spglib/spglib-1.16.2/doc/definition.rst)). Third, the origin floats in polar classes: along a line in classes 2, 3, 4, 6, mm2, 3m, 4mm and 6mm, in a plane in class m, and in all of space in class 1 [S] ([IUCr Dictionary: Normalizer](http://reference.iucr.org/dictionary/Normalizer)). Dauter marks these directions with an ε and places the molecular centre at 0 along them, which is the same convention as your polar-origin fixing [V] ([PMC4005360](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4005360/)).

The nearest published object to "asymmetric unit of N_E⁺(G)" comes from macromolecular crystallography. **Dauter's "anti-Cheshire" symmetry is the chirality-preserving normaliser.** He proposed its asymmetric unit as a standard placement region, and Kowiel, Jaskolski & Dauter implemented it in the ACHESYM server [V for Dauter; S/V for ACHESYM] ([PMC4005360](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4005360/); [Kowiel et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1107/S1399004714024572)). That is a convention for placing a deposited model, not a search domain. It also places only the molecular centre and leaves orientation untouched. No source located a general tabulation of N_E asymmetric units for all 230 groups. ITA tabulates the normalisers themselves, and the exact direct-space asymmetric units of Grosse-Kunstleve et al. (2011) cover only the space groups [K] ([IUCr page](https://journals.iucr.org/a/issues/2011/03/00/pz5088/index.html)).

## Each factor has a settled name; "Cheshire cell" and "chiral group" are traps

The terminology splits by community. Crystallographers say **asymmetric unit**, mathematicians say **fundamental domain/region**, texture analysts say **fundamental zone**, and MR uses **unique region of the rotation function**. A materials reader who sees "fundamental domain" will assume you mean the space group's asymmetric unit in direct space, not a domain in the space of crystal descriptions. Hirshfeld himself wrote "asymmetric unit" for his 6-D domain. "Fundamental domain" is mathematically correct for your object. It is used in crystallography for the reduced-cell region (Andrews–Bernstein) [S] and in ML theory by Aslan, Platt & Sheard [V] ([PMLR 197](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v197/aslan23a.html)). Define it explicitly at first use. No crystal generative model we checked uses the phrase for its sampling domain [V across the fetched papers].

Two further notes on terms. ITA, Bilbao and the ML papers spell it "normalizer", so search with that spelling. The MR rotation-function asymmetric unit is a **double coset**, with the crystal's proper Laue rotations on the left and the model's self-rotation on the right, and its redundancy factor is the product of the two orders. That makes it a closer analogue of your orientation factor than a plain SO(3)/P_mol quotient [V] ([Yeates lab](https://yeateslab.mbi.ucla.edu/solution-of-the-rotation-function-asymmetric-unit-problem); [Rao, Jih & Hartsuck 1980](https://typeset.io/pdf/rotation-function-space-groups-1ne3a7h92l.pdf)).

## The product is a valid fundamental domain except on measure-zero sets

Two statements about product structure appear in the material. Both are true and they do not conflict. Hirshfeld's "direct product" is a statement about the **group**: the Cheshire group acts on position and, through its linear part, on orientation from the left, and the molecular point group acts on orientation from the right, so the two actions commute. One note infers that the 6-D asymmetric unit of that group is not in general a Cartesian product, because normaliser rotations move orientation and position together [inference, unverified against the full text]. That is correct as a statement that the action does not factor into independent position and orientation actions. It does not rule out a product-shaped domain. A generic centroid position has a trivial stabiliser in N_E. Each orbit therefore contains, generically, exactly one representative whose centroid lies in the N_E asymmetric unit; orientation is carried along by that unique normaliser element, and the only residual freedom is the right action of the molecular point group, which leaves the centroid fixed. So **(asymmetric unit of N_E acting on position) × (orientation fundamental zone under the molecular point group) is a valid fundamental domain up to measure-zero sets**: the representative is chosen by position, and orientation follows.

The product fails cleanly on three kinds of set, and these matter for canonicalisation and seam handling more than for measure. First, centroids on the boundary of the N_E asymmetric unit or on special positions have nontrivial stabilisers, and there the orientation must be further quotiented by the stabiliser. Second, **N_E enlarges at specialised metrics**, for example a = c in monoclinic or a = b in orthorhombic, so on those cell-parameter boundaries the true quotient is by a larger group and the product over-counts [S] ([Bilbao enhanced normaliser](https://cryst.ehu.es/cryst/get_nor.html)). Third, the fibre over the lattice factor is constant only on the open set of general metric. This makes your domain a bundle over the reduced-cell domain whose fibre group jumps on measure-zero metric strata, rather than a single global product.

Two further points shape how the domain behaves in use. Seams are unavoidable: MTEX warns that projecting to a fundamental region makes nearby orientations map to distant representatives [S] ([MTEX](https://mtex-toolbox.github.io/OrientationFundamentalRegion.html)). CG-OMatG proves that no single-valued equivariant orientation map exists when the molecular stabiliser is nontrivial [V] ([arXiv 2609.39773](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.39773)). For Z′ > 1, MR offers a precedent: the normaliser is used once to fix the origin with the first molecule, and later molecules are searched over the full or primitive cell [V] ([CCP4](https://staraniso.globalphasing.org/html/cheshirecell.html)).

As a labelled inference, not drawn from a source: the lattice factor for a fixed space group is a quotient by the linear parts of the **affine** normaliser N_A(G), meaning the basis changes that preserve G in its setting. Niggli/Křivý–Gruber reduction is exactly that quotient only in the triclinic case. Check this against ITA ch. 3.5 before writing "Niggli-reduced" for monoclinic and higher systems.

## Search tools sample the full cell and deduplicate after minimisation

No classical, random, evolutionary or powder search code we checked restricts the representative molecule's position by the normaliser. The best-documented case is **GLEE**. It samples each centroid over [0,1)³ in every space group, says explicitly that it keeps translations along polar axes, uses Shoemake quaternions over all of SO(3), and cycles through the orderings of cell lengths rather than reducing them. It then clusters minimised structures on Niggli-reduced cells with a COMPACK-like overlay that tries S symmetry overlays [V] ([Case et al. 2016, PMC4750085](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4750085)). **PyXtal** handles site symmetry carefully and removes redundant discrete orientations using the molecular point group. Its free 3-DOF rotation draw is not Haar-uniform, however: a cube-normalised axis is combined with a uniform angle. Its normaliser machinery, `get_alternatives()`, enumerates alternative Wyckoff settings for group–subgroup analysis, not for search [V code] ([molecule.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MaterSim/PyXtal/master/pyxtal/molecule.py); [pyxtal/__init__.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MaterSim/PyXtal/master/pyxtal/__init__.py)). Zhu's group states that alternative Wyckoff settings come from the Euclidean and affine normalisers [S] ([Zhu, Kang & Parrish 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.10655)). The 6th blind test lists grid, random, Sobol and simulated-annealing generators across all teams, and its main text never mentions origin, normaliser or reduced-cell restriction [V] ([Reilly et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4971545)).

The MR and powder precedent is sharper than the CSP precedent, and narrower than your construction. **AMoRe, Phaser (`TRANslate FULL`, documented as the Cheshire-cell or primitive-cell volume) and TFFC bound the first molecule's translation search by the Cheshire cell** [V] ([Phaser 2.1 keywords](https://www-structmed.cimr.cam.ac.uk/phaser_obsolete/documentation/phaser-2.1_key.html); [CCP4](https://staraniso.globalphasing.org/html/cheshirecell.html)). Rotation searches use the separate rotation-function asymmetric unit (Tollin, Main & Rossmann 1966; Rao et al. 1980; Moss 1985). Yeates (1993) solved the cubic and icosahedral cases with Dirichlet domains in rotation-matrix space [V/S] ([Yeates lab](https://yeateslab.mbi.ucla.edu/solution-of-the-rotation-function-asymmetric-unit-problem)).

MR never quotients by the normaliser's point operations, because the rotation function fixes orientation and handedness first. Normaliser rotations that are absent from the Laue group appear in MR as **alternative indexing**, such as h,k,−l for P4 and P6, and are resolved from data [V] ([Dauter 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4005360/)). Energy-only CSP has no data to break that degeneracy, so **your joint quotient over (orientation, position) is strictly finer than standard MR practice**. The 6-D stochastic MR programs do not reduce their domain either. EPMR draws positions over the unit cell and quaternions over all of SO(3), and its full text contains no Cheshire or normaliser term [V] ([EPMR](https://people.mbi.ucla.edu/sawaya/tutorials/Molrep/manuals/epmr.pdf)). Queen of Spades assigns random orientations and translations with no bounds stated [V] ([Qs manual](https://utopia.duth.gr/glykos/Qs-html/node23.html)). Powder direct-space codes reach at most the space group's own asymmetric unit, as in ObjCryst++'s `AsymmetricUnit` box [S] ([ObjCryst++ source](https://objcryst.readthedocs.io/en/latest/a00134_source.html)).

Where the normaliser does appear in this literature, it is used for post-search comparison: phenix.find_alt_orig_sym_mate searches symmetry operations × alternative origins and slides along polar axes [V] ([Phenix docs](https://phenix-online.org/documentation/reference/find_alt_orig_sym_mate.html)), and Bilbao's COMPSTRU explores N_E descriptions [S] ([COMPSTRU](https://cryst.ehu.es/cryst/help/compstru.html)). Chirikjian's "motion space" series formalises MR search spaces as coset spaces of SE(3) and is the closest formal treatment of a 6-D fundamental domain [S] ([Chirikjian & Yan, Part II](https://rpk.lcsr.jhu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/Chirikjian12_Math-Aspects-II-Geometry.pdf); [Shiffman, Lyu & Chirikjian 2020 via Semantic Scholar](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1107/S0567739468000501/citations?fields=title,year,authors,venue,externalIds&limit=200)).

Hirshfeld's influence on CSP effectively stopped around 2005. Semantic Scholar lists 83 citing records. The CSP-relevant ones are Dzyabchenko's lattice-energy searches (1984–2004), van Eijck & Kroon's 1997 "Fast clustering of equivalent structures", and Papoular et al. (2005). No post-2005 CSP or ML citer appears [V for the citation links only, not their content] ([Semantic Scholar](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1107/S0567739468000501/citations?fields=title,year,authors,venue,externalIds&limit=200)). Dzyabchenko's grid program is the most likely place to find an older, unnamed normaliser reduction. It is an unread lead.

## Generative models canonicalise lattices but never quotient the normaliser

Inorganic full-cell generators handle the redundancies in a consistent way, and none of them builds a quotient domain. Rotation is handled by representation (lengths and angles, a Gram matrix, or a symmetric polar factor). Origin is handled by invariance on the torus. Lattice basis is handled by Niggli canonicalisation. DiffCSP calls its fractional domain a quotient space [V] ([DiffCSP](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.04475)). FlowMM picks the Niggli-unique cell, which confines angles to 60°–120° [V] ([FlowMM](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.04713)). MatterGen Niggli-reduces and then polar-decomposes [V] ([model card](https://huggingface.co/microsoft/mattergen)), and ADiT does the same reduction [V] ([ADiT](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.03965)). Symmetry-aware models remove space-group redundancy but inherit the normaliser coset. **SGEquiDiff uses exact space-group asymmetric-unit polytopes, built on Grosse-Kunstleve's face, edge and vertex conditions. This is the closest ML construction to a true fundamental domain, but it belongs to G, not N_E** [V] ([arXiv 2505.10994](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.10994)). Only the Wyckoff-token line names the normaliser, and it handles it by enumeration or augmentation:

- Wyckoff Transformer isolates the coset-representative choice in an enumeration index and randomises the residual each training epoch [V] ([arXiv 2503.02407](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.02407v4)).
- Yamazaki et al. enumerate Bilbao coset representatives for augmentation [V] ([arXiv 2503.16784](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16784)).
- MaskGXT augments with a continuous origin shift relabelled by precomputed normaliser translation cosets for all 230 groups [V] ([arXiv 2606.22866](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.22866v1)).
- CrystalFormer lists the normaliser as future work [V] ([arXiv 2403.15734](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.15734)).

The 2025–2026 molecular-crystal generators work in P1 cells or lattice-free clusters:

- MolCrystalFlow uses Z ∈ {2,4} in P1 with QR-normalised lattices and axis-flip states [V] ([arXiv 2602.16020](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16020v3)).
- CG-OMatG gives the most complete symmetry inventory and names the quotient, but chooses equivariance over quotient modelling [V] ([arXiv 2609.39773](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.39773)).
- Clari is all-atom Cartesian [V] ([arXiv 2606.03199](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.03199)).

## Conclusion

The novelty is narrower and more defensible than "a fundamental domain for molecular crystals". Hirshfeld owns the position-and-orientation quotient at fixed cell, and Andrews–Bernstein own the reduced-cell region. What no one has published, within the search budget, is four things together: the bundle of the reduced-cell domain with the normaliser asymmetric unit and the orientation fundamental zone; the specialised-metric strata on which the fibre group jumps; the chirality-preserving variant for chiral molecules; and the use of that domain as the support of a sampler. Framing the contribution as "Hirshfeld's 1968 asymmetric unit, extended over the lattice and made exact for chiral molecules and polar groups" will read as scholarship rather than reinvention to crystallographers. It will also give inorganic-ML readers the vocabulary they already use: normaliser cosets, alternative settings and Wyckoff sets.

The practical gap runs the other way too. Every working CSP search quotients after minimisation and accepts an [N_E : G] × |P_mol| × Z-fold redundant generator plus continuous polar drift. That is cheap for single-optimum searches but costly for basin counting and density estimation. The open risk sits in your own domain's seams, not in its definition. Boundary identifications on the Niggli cone, normaliser enlargement at specialised metrics, and the impossibility of a seam-free orientation chart are exactly where a learned sampler will see discontinuities, and they deserve explicit tests.
